## 1. Purpose and scope

Turn the element system into one composed design for the actual physical piece. The output is an **Execution Specification**: a clear account of what the viewer sees and in what order, how space is organized, how every element is treated in its role, and how the whole behaves under real viewing conditions. This phase decides arrangement and relationships. It does not yet phrase anything for the image model.

## 2. Establishing visual hierarchy

Hierarchy is the order in which the audience should notice, interpret, and act. Derive it from the Communication Problem, not from convention.

1. **State the message in one sentence** the viewer should take away at the first glance. If it needs two sentences, the piece has two jobs; decide which leads.
2. **Define the reading levels the piece needs.** Some pieces need a single dominant level and a quiet remainder; others need a catalog structure where many items are equally scannable; others need a sequence. Choose the number and nature of levels the content demands. Do not impose a standard count.
3. **Place all content, text and imagery together, into those levels.** The hero, the mark, the headline, the price, the tagline, the supporting description, and the contact details are competing for position. Determine which are co-dominant, which are subordinate, which are reference information consulted only after interest is earned.
4. **Assign dominance by multiple means**, not by size alone: scale, contrast, color, isolation, position, weight, density of surroundings. Hierarchy built only on size is brittle and crude.
5. **Match the hierarchy to the viewing conditions.** What must be perceived at the farthest realistic distance, and what may wait for a closer look?
6. **Treat the logo mark as a deliberate hierarchy decision.** It may lead, anchor, or sign off, depending on the piece's job. Its prominence must reflect its job, not habit.

Test: can you describe the piece by the order of what the eye finds? If the order is ambiguous or contradictory, hierarchy is unresolved.

## 3. Developing composition and layout

Derive the spatial organization from medium, aspect ratio, content, hierarchy, and style direction. Do not import a stock layout.

Decide:
- **Spatial strategy**: how the format is organized (a single field, zoned areas, bands, a focal center with satellites, a modular catalog structure, a flowing continuous surface). Choose because the content and viewing demand it.
- **Zones and groupings**: which content belongs together by function, which must be separated, and how groupings are made evident (proximity, alignment, shared treatment, containment).
- **Scale relationships**: the ratio between dominant and supporting elements, large enough to create clarity, not so extreme that supporting content becomes unreadable.
- **Alignment and structure**: the underlying order, whether strict, relaxed, or deliberately broken, that gives the layout its logic. It should be felt, not necessarily visible.
- **Negative space**: where the eye rests and what that rest communicates. Breathing room is a design element with a purpose; it is not leftover area. Distinguish intentional emptiness from unplanned gaps.
- **Density and rhythm**: how often emphasis recurs, how information is paced, and whether the density suits the viewing time.
- **Directional flow**: the path the eye takes from first to last element, helped by orientation, gaze or gesture of depicted figures, lines, alignment, and weight distribution.
- **Foreground and background relationship**: depth, overlap, and containment.
- **Aspect ratio behavior**: extreme horizontal or vertical formats need specific strategies for flow and scale. A tall narrow format reads in sequence; a very wide format reads as a band and tolerates distance; a near-square format tolerates central organization but risks static composition.
- **Edge behavior**: whether elements bleed, are contained, or sit within a margin, and how important content stays safe from edges and trimming.

Avoid a universal grid, a fixed number of zones, or a default center-aligned stack. If the composition could be applied to any business by swapping text, it has not been designed.

## 4. Implementing the elements

For each element, determine its expression within its compositional role: scale, visual weight, color, treatment, position, and relationship to neighbors.

- **Typography** follows the plan's voice and the hierarchy: each text category has a defined role, scale relationship, and alignment behavior. Prices, calls to action, and contact details are functional text and must remain unambiguous.
- **The mark** is placed with enough clear space to be recognized independently and sized to its hierarchy role.
- **The hero** is scaled and cropped to serve the message, not merely to fill space; consider whether cropping, overlap, or bleeding strengthens or weakens recognition.
- **Supporters** sit at a lower level than the hero and mark and relate to the content they serve.
- **Graphic devices** reinforce grouping and rhythm rather than adding noise.

Elements must interact as an art-directed whole: shared edges, repeated proportions, echoed shapes, and consistent treatment. An assembly of well-made objects is not a design.

## 5. Implementing backgrounds and transitions

Make the background work across the piece:
- Ensure it supports contrast where text lives.
- Use continuity to unify zones and contrast to separate them.
- Plan transitions between major areas: how one zone hands off to the next through a change in tone, a shared device, or a deliberate break.
- Keep supporting treatments subordinate to the hierarchy.
- Check for edge-to-edge consistency, so the piece does not look like separate designs joined together.

## 6. Accounting for physical print conditions

Evaluate the design as a physical object seen in its setting.

- **Viewing distance**: legibility thresholds rise with distance; the smallest important text must be defensible at the intended distance. If it is not, change its size, simplify the content, or reorganize.
- **Ambient light**: bright outdoor light, shade, dim interiors, warm artificial light, and glare change how color and contrast read. Favor contrast structures that survive the actual light.
- **Material and finish**: absorbent stock, glossy surfaces, textured paper, flexible vinyl, adhesive stock, curved packaging, or fabric each affect fine detail, color saturation, and edge behavior. Align the style to what the material will reproduce.
- **Production limits**: low-cost or high-volume printing tolerates fewer subtle gradients, very thin lines, tiny reversed text, and delicate textures. Decide whether the design needs to be robust to imperfect reproduction.
- **Handling and exposure**: pieces that are touched, folded, wet, or sun-exposed need resilient treatments.
- **Format-specific behavior**: the physical form (folds, panels, curved surfaces, window placement, hanging orientation) changes what is visible and when.

Separate **visual design direction** from **technical production specification**. The prompt should convey what affects the look and the generation, namely flat artwork, format proportions, and critical visual traits. It should not carry bleed values, color profiles, resolution settings, or printer instructions unless the user requests them or they materially change the image.

## 7. Multi-face and multi-panel pieces

- Plan the set as a system: the shared identity, a consistent logic, and distinct jobs per face.
- Define the face the viewer meets first and its hierarchy; later faces reward interest with information rather than repeating the first.
- Keep treatment consistent across faces (color logic, type voice, mark use, edge behavior) while varying composition according to each face's job.
- Plan the continuity between panels where the piece unfolds, and what is visible when it is closed.
- Each face receives its own prompt, restating the shared identity completely.

## 8. Reviewing the complete composition

Evaluate the assembled design from each team perspective, in the frame of the Communication Problem. At minimum:

- **Message**: is the primary message understood at first glance?
- **Hierarchy**: does the order support the intended audience response?
- **Identity**: are the logo mark and brand distinctive and aligned with the brand design?
- **Concept**: does the design express the core idea consistently?
- **Contribution**: does every visual element add value?
- **Legibility**: is mandatory content legible and appropriately prioritized under real conditions?
- **Balance and completeness**: does the composition feel intentional and finished?
- **Detail**: do the background and micro-details enhance without distracting?
- **Specificity**: is it tailored to this business and print context?
- **Authenticity**: does it avoid generic, templated, overdecorated, or AI-typical appearance?

Use the tests and severity mapping in `quality_control.md`. If a criterion fails, return to the earliest cause (hierarchy, element system, plan) rather than adjusting the surface.

## 9. Failure modes

| Failure | Detection | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Flat hierarchy | Everything equal; no first read | Re-rank content; differentiate by contrast and isolation |
| Size-only hierarchy | Emphasis created only by scaling up | Add contrast, color, isolation, position |
| Stock layout | Layout unchanged when content swapped | Re-derive from content and hierarchy |
| Crammed content | No resting space; small text everywhere | Reduce, regroup, or restructure |
| Empty for its own sake | Large unplanned gaps | Assign function to space or fill with purposeful content |
| Distance blindness | Key text unreadable from the real distance | Rescale or simplify |
| Mark buried | Mark overlaps decoration or loses clear space | Isolate and resize |
| Disconnected zones | Zones look like separate designs | Add continuity or unify treatment |
| Production fantasy | Treatments the user's printing cannot hold | Adapt style to the material |

## 10. Completion criteria and hand-off

Complete when the Execution Specification states: the one-sentence message; the hierarchy and reading order; the spatial organization and groupings; each element's scale, weight, color, treatment, and position relative to others; background and transition behavior; how the design responds to the physical conditions; and the review has passed.

Hand-off to Phase 6: the Execution Specification, the exact mandatory text and where each item sits, and a short list of the traits that make the design distinctive and must not be lost.
