## 1. Process

1. State the one-sentence message the viewer takes away first.
2. Assign every content item to a reading stage (B.1).
3. Decide the space plan before placing anything (B.2).
4. Decide the spatial strategy by the protocol (C).
5. Rank and place content (A), sizing each tier to its legibility minimum plus hierarchy needs, and triaging if content exceeds the space plan (B.4 and B.5).
6. Implement elements (D) and backgrounds and transitions (E).
7. Account for physical print conditions (F) and multi-face structure (G).
8. Review the complete composition (H). On failure, return to the earliest responsible decision and re-run forward.
9. Pass the gate; record the decision-summary entry.

The order in step 2 to 5 is fixed: reading stages, then space plan, then sizes, then placement. The spatial strategy is decided after the space plan because the strategy must arrange content within the space already reserved, not renegotiate it.

## 2. Detailed guidance

### A. Visual hierarchy
Determine the order in which the audience should notice, interpret, and use the information. Determine how many levels the piece needs (there is no standard count). Rank text and imagery together. Assign dominance by multiple means: scale, contrast, color, isolation, position, weight. The mark's prominence matches its Phase 3 role, and its distinction is secured by mechanisms (Phase 4), not size alone.

### B. Scale, reading stages, space, and density
Visibility requirements guide prioritization; they do not entitle every element to be large.

1. **Assign every content item to a reading stage** by when the viewer needs it, not by how important the owner feels it is. The stages come from the Environment Profile's viewing stages. Only glance-stage content is sized for the farthest distance. Record each item's stage and tier.
2. **Decide the space plan before placing anything,** as proportions of the format: an **outer margin** that scales with the format (large formats need proportionally wide margins); **isolation zones** around the glance content, hero, and mark; **separations** between content groups (space is the first separator, ahead of rules and boxes); **resting fields** positioned to support the eye's path. Place content into what remains. If it does not fit, change the content (triage), the grouping, or the sizes; never the space plan. The plan must honor the Phase 3 space intent.
3. **Respect the medium's scale.** A larger medium has more area but not a different viewer: it serves a farther viewer (the glance tier grows and the rest stays modest) or lets the same content breathe. It rarely justifies more elements. When content seems small for the area, the answers are a bolder single idea, a larger glance tier, or more space, never more items.
4. **Treat legibility as a minimum.** Set a legibility minimum per tier from stage distance, material, light, and contrast. Size beyond the minimum buys emphasis, which works only when most elements do not have it.
5. **Budget the content.** Count groups and items per tier. The number of competing groups at one stage should be what the viewer can parse in that stage's time. If content exceeds the space plan, apply **triage**: demote items to a later stage, group, simplify, or omit optional content. Never delete mandatory content, shrink below the minimum, inflate every tier, or reduce the space plan. If triage cannot make mandatory content fit, record a volume risk for the notes and keep the content.
6. **Use space as visibility** (isolation raises glance visibility more reliably than size) and **express scale as relationships** (proportions and steps between tiers).
7. **Check.** Name each element's stage and minimum; reduce any element far above its minimum that is neither the hero nor the glance message. Run the **fill test** on each quiet area: name its job; an area with a job is kept; an area with none is reassigned in the plan, never filled with an item.

### C. Composition and layout
Compare at least three structurally different spatial strategies under the protocol. Structural difference means different organizing logic (where the eye enters, how groups relate, how the hero and information divide the format), not different positions of the same arrangement. Establish zones, groupings, alignments, scale relationships, negative space (from the space plan), density and rhythm, directional flow, foreground and background relationship, aspect-ratio behavior, and edge behavior. Judge density, scale, and finish relative to the surroundings. No universal grid, fixed number of zones, or stock layout without a compelling reason. Extreme aspect ratios change how the eye travels; decide the path explicitly.

### D. Implementing elements
Express each element in its role: scale, weight, color, treatment, position, relationship. Typography sizes follow reading stages. Prices, calls to action, and contact details are functional text, clear at their own stage, not enlarged by default. The mark claims its own region with protected clear space. Elements interact as one art-directed whole. Specify where every mandatory text item sits and in what typographic role.

### E. Backgrounds and transitions
Continuity and contrast across zones, transitions between major areas, subordination to hierarchy, edge-to-edge consistency. Backgrounds stay quiet where information lives.

### F. Physical print conditions
Viewing distance per stage, ambient light, material and finish, production limits, handling and exposure, format behavior (folds, panels, curvature). Separate visual direction from technical production specifications; carry forward only what changes the look or the generation.

### G. Multi-face and multi-panel pieces
Plan the set as a system with a shared identity and a distinct job per face. Keep treatment consistent, plan continuity between panels, and prepare one standalone prompt per face. Establish the shared identity once; decide reading-stage assignment, space plan, and spatial strategy for each face, because faces have different jobs and possibly different viewing conditions.

### H. Review of the complete composition
Review from each team perspective. Assess: message at first glance; hierarchy; distinctive mark and identity (including the text-neighborhood test); concept consistency; contribution of every element; legibility; balance and completeness; background and detail; specificity to this business and context; proportion and space; fit among actual neighbors and light with departure on the intended dimension; absence of generic, templated, overdecorated, or AI-typical appearance. On failure, name the earliest responsible decision (plan, element, or placement), return there, and re-run forward; cap at two cycles per issue.

## 3. Failure modes

| Failure | Detection | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Abstract hierarchy | Importance assigned by owner preference, not by stage | Reassign by when the viewer needs each item |
| Size inflation | Many elements far above their minimums | Reduce non-glance elements toward minimums |
| Filling space | A quiet area with no job | Run the fill test; reassign the area in the plan |
| More area, more items | Content added because the format is large | Strengthen the main idea, grow the glance tier, or add space |
| Space plan shrunk to fit | Margins or isolation reduced to hold content | Restore the plan; triage content |
| Mandatory content deleted | A mandatory string missing from the specification | Restore it; regroup or rescale instead |
| Mark swallowed | Fails the text-neighborhood test in composition | Give it its own region; add mechanisms; change neighboring type |
| One-axis hierarchy | Dominance created by size alone | Add contrast, isolation, position, weight, color |
| Strategies that are one layout | Three "strategies" with the same organizing logic | Rewrite on structurally different logics |
| Fit without departure, or departure without fit | Review shows blending into neighbors, or alienating them | Re-balance on the chosen dimension |
| Stock layout | The composition would work for any business | Re-derive from the core idea and the Environment Profile |
| Dense text ignored | Dense content with no grouping or row behavior | Group in reading order; describe rows once |

## 4. Completion criteria, summary record, and hand-off

Complete when the phase card's "Before leaving" check holds.

**Record for the decision summary (Design Execution block):** the reading-stage assignment in brief (what is at glance, approach, close inspection) and the space plan with proportions; the spatial strategy (candidates compared, choice, decisive criterion, runner-up). State that the reading-stage assignment and space plan were decided by rule, not by candidate comparison. Update the Design Log: reading stages, space plan, open issues.

**Hand-off to Phase 6:** the Execution Specification: the message; hierarchy and reading order; reading-stage assignment; space plan with proportions; spatial strategy; placement, scale, weight, color, and treatment of every element and text item (including mandatory text); background and transition behavior; print-condition adjustments; the review result.
