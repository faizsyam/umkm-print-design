# Phase 2: Analysis

## Phase card

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Professional analogue | Strategic discovery and planning: a brand strategist and researcher who establish the real problem and the real setting before anyone sketches |
| Goal | Understand the business, audience, environment, and the piece's actual job well enough that every later decision can be justified by a finding; reduce that understanding to design implications plus two creative inputs, the Environment Profile and the Visual source inventory |

## 1. Process

1. Triage the research questions: name the decision each informs; drop those that inform none.
2. Understand the brand beyond its description (A).
3. Find differentiation: list candidate differentiators, evaluate each for truth, relevance, distinctiveness, and visualizability, choose a strategy (or execution-led differentiation if none is strong), and record the options weighed (B).
4. Build the Environment Profile (C).
5. Build the audience encounter (D).
6. Define the Communication Problem, including its weakest link and tensions (E).
7. Research per F, evaluating source reliability and confirming that a found business is the right one.
8. Ground culturally and build the Visual source inventory (G).
9. Convert findings into design implications. Discard any finding that yields none. Carry the Phase 1 quality translations forward as given implications.
10. Pass the gate. Guided mode: print the Phase 2 entry (`00_conversation_ux.md` section 5), ask for confirmation, and stop; Phase 3 begins on the go-ahead. Auto mode: print nothing and continue.

Steps 3 to 8 interleave in practice; the order above is the order of conclusions, not of fact-finding. Research early enough that the Environment Profile and inventory rest on it.

## 2. Detailed guidance

Every part below uses the same five blocks: **Purpose**, **Analyze**, **Consider**, **Evaluate**, **Record**. Work through each list; a part is complete when its Record items exist.

### A. The brand beyond its description

**Purpose.** Read the brand as it truly is, so identity decisions rest on evidence rather than on an invented narrative.

**Analyze**
- Values, personality, and character as shown in what the business does and says.
- Core message and positioning.
- Sources of credibility the business actually holds (origin, skill, history, proof supplied).
- Aspirations the owner expresses.
- The relationship with customers (regular, one-off, community) and its intimacy.

**Consider**
- How much evidence exists for each trait.
- The owner's own voice and register in the materials supplied.
- The business's scale and how that shapes what can honestly be claimed.

**Evaluate**
- Which traits are established facts and which are plausible interpretations.
- Which are aspiration rather than current reality.
- What the brand can truthfully claim visually.
- Whether any part of the reading is richer than its evidence; if so, downgrade it.

**Record**
- A provenance-tagged brand reading, no richer than its evidence.
- The traits that must be expressed and the claims that must not be made.

### B. Differentiation

**Purpose.** Find what genuinely sets this business apart in this landscape, and decide how the design carries it.

**Analyze**
- Candidate differentiators from: product specifics, process, materials, origin, scale and intimacy, service behavior, price logic, location, people, name, signature item, consumption ritual.
- The local competitive landscape and what each neighbor already claims.

**Consider**
- Which differentiators the audience can verify at the moment of encounter.
- Positioning that fits dozens of competitors (avoid it).
- Whether the real difference lies in execution rather than in any single claim.

**Evaluate** each candidate differentiator on four tests:
- **True:** the business can honestly claim it. A failure discards it.
- **Relevant:** the audience cares at the moment of encounter.
- **Distinctive:** not shared by dozens of competitors.
- **Visualizable:** it can become something seen. A failure may still shape tone or content hierarchy.

Choose a strategy; if none is strong, choose execution-led differentiation (the piece differs through how it is composed and made) and say so.

**Record**
- The differentiators weighed, with the result of each test.
- The chosen strategy, or the statement that evidence fixed it.

### C. The Environment Profile (a governing design parameter)

**Purpose.** Reconstruct the real encounter so that the setting generates and constrains design decisions in every later phase.

**Analyze**
- Physical setting and surrounding visual landscape (dominant colors, density, scale, order or clutter, finish level).
- Competing stimuli and what they promise.
- Ambient light and conditions.
- Audience behavior there: moving or still, attention, dwell time.
- Cultural context of the place.
- Viewing stages (glance, approach, close inspection; a piece may have only one), each with distance and duration.

**Consider**
- Impression versus expression: what the landscape already says, and what expression would still carry the intended impression there. Hold impression constant; let the setting decide expression.
- Where neighbors are uniform (color field, density, scale, form language, finish, tone) and where fitting is required for credibility or appropriateness.
- That louder is not the same as distinct; quieter can be the departure in a loud landscape.
- That departure serves visibility and never overrides what the brief asks the piece to look and feel like; the same dimension can usually be expressed in a way that keeps every quality intact.

**Evaluate**
- The departure dimension(s), chosen by weighing real options against the actual landscape and screened for compatibility with every quality requirement. An option that would contradict one is ineligible; choose another dimension or another expression of it.
- The fit dimensions that must be respected.
- Whether the setting is established or must be marked provisional (state the most probable setting if it cannot be established).

**Record**
- The profile in concrete terms, with departure and fit dimensions.
- Downstream use: a candidate source and scoring criterion in Phase 3; the basis for color, typographic, mark, and background judgments in Phase 4; the basis for viewing stages, density, and the final context review in Phase 5.

### D. The audience

**Purpose.** Understand how these people actually meet the piece, so the design answers their behavior, not a stereotype.

**Analyze**
- Cultural references and expectations.
- Behavior, motivations, needs, and purchasing considerations.
- The language register they use and trust.
- Trust cues that the setting already provides or lacks.

**Consider**
- At each viewing stage: what earns attention, what makes the offer relevant, what builds confidence, what prompts action.
- What the audience already knows about the category and about this business.

**Evaluate**
- Which audience claims rest on evidence and which on assumption; drop demographic stereotypes and unsupported psychology.
- What the audience must conclude at each viewing stage for the piece to work.

**Record**
- The audience encounter as a short sequence of what they do and decide at each viewing stage.

### E. The Communication Problem

**Purpose.** Define the piece's job precisely, so every later decision can be tested against it.

**Analyze**
- Primary and secondary objectives. A priority the user states (what must come first) fixes the primary objective; the weakest link is chosen within it and never replaces it.
- The quality requirements from Phase 1, as part of the desired response and brand perception.
- The desired audience response.
- The necessary information.
- The desired brand perception.

**Consider** the tensions, and list each:
- visibility versus sophistication;
- density versus readability;
- memorability versus commercial clarity;
- legibility distance versus content volume versus area;
- ambition versus production budget.

**Evaluate**
- The **weakest link** in attention → comprehension → credibility → action: the link most likely to fail in this setting, chosen by weighing the options and the cost of each failure.

**Record**
- The Communication Problem, the weakest link with options weighed, and the tension list. Leave resolution of the tensions to Phase 3.

### F. Research

**Purpose.** Replace assumption with evidence, only where evidence can change a decision.

**Analyze** (in priority order)
- The actual business and its public materials.
- Category visual conventions to differentiate from.
- Local competitive context.
- The environment.
- Cultural references.
- Product reality.

**Consider**
- Source reliability: prefer authoritative and first-hand sources; treat reviews as signals of perception, not verified fact.
- Whether a found business is the right one (the same name is not the same business).
- Tool availability: if research tools are unavailable, reason from the brief, mark conclusions provisional, and avoid choices that depend on unverified specifics.

**Evaluate**
- Whether each finding changes or confirms a decision; stop when further research would not.
- That competitor visuals are studied as a baseline to differ from, never copied.

**Record**
- Findings with provenance tags, and the decision each informs.

### G. Cultural grounding and the Visual source inventory

**Purpose.** Ground the design in true, specific, respectful visual sources beyond the emblem everyone reaches for first.

**Analyze**
- The cultures, languages, religions, and commercial conventions actually relevant; do not treat "Indonesian" as one aesthetic or audience.
- The user's language register (preserve it).
- Regional or traditional references, used only where the business genuinely connects to them.

**Consider** the deeper specifics, across seven dimensions:
- practices and rituals;
- materials and tools;
- how things are made, handled, and shared;
- spatial and sensory details of daily life;
- language and its play;
- geography and seasons;
- the history of this business.

**Evaluate**
- Truth: can the business honestly claim it?
- Respect: is it free of tokenism?
- Meaning: verify any culturally loaded symbol; omit it if uncertain.
- Visual workability: can it be seen, drawn, and rendered at the viewing distance?

**Record** the inventory in three parts, each entry grounded in the brief or research:
1. The apparent associations, named as the baseline.
2. Deeper true specifics that could become visual ideas.
3. Which of those are visually workable.

## 3. Failure modes

| Failure | Detection | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Research for its own sake | Findings that change no decision | Drop them; keep only those with an implication |
| Narrative richer than evidence | Brand claims with no source | Downgrade to modest reading; tag as inference |
| Category-level differentiation | The strategy fits dozens of competitors | Re-run B with this business's specifics or choose execution-led |
| Abstract environment | The profile could describe any location | Add concrete neighbors, light, distance, behavior; mark provisional if unknown |
| Loud equals distinct | Departure chosen as simply "bigger or brighter" | Re-weigh departure dimensions against the actual landscape |
| Inventory of emblems | Part 2 contains only the apparent associations or their variants | Mine the seven dimensions again; require at least one true specific beyond the baseline |
| Unverified symbol | A culturally loaded element with no checked meaning | Verify or omit |
| Competitor copying | A finding about a competitor's visuals carried forward as a model rather than a baseline to differ from | Re-state it as a departure dimension; never reproduce it |
| Manufactured alternatives | Differentiation options that are one idea reworded | State that evidence leaves one defensible option |
| Stereotyped audience | Demographic assumptions without evidence | Replace with observed or inferred behavior at each viewing stage |
| Departure overrides the brief | The chosen departure signals the opposite of a stated quality | Re-weigh; choose a compatible dimension or expression |
| Weakest link displaces the user's priority | The primary objective differs from what the user said comes first | Restore the stated priority; use the weakest link as a secondary lens |

## 4. Completion criteria, summary record, and hand-off

Complete when the phase card's "Before leaving" check holds.

**Print for the decision summary (Analysis entry, at the gate):** the weakest link and the options weighed; the departure and fit dimensions chosen and why; the differentiation strategy and options weighed (or "fixed by evidence"). Mark any decision the evidence fixed as such. Update the Design Log: Communication Problem, Environment Profile, Visual source inventory, open issues.

**Hand-off to Phase 3:** Communication Problem; Environment Profile; Visual source inventory; differentiation strategy; tagged design implications; tension list; plus Phase 1's content volume, fixed assets, preferences, and exclusions.
