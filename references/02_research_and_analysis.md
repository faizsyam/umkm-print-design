## 1. Purpose and scope

Build a context-specific understanding of the business and the conditions in which the piece must work, then reduce it to a small set of **design implications** that Phase 3 can act on. Analysis that cannot be converted into an implication is discarded. This phase answers *what is the real problem and what does it demand*, not *what should it look like*.

## 2. Deciding whether and what to research

Research is justified when an answer could change a decision. Before each search, name the decision it informs. If no decision is named, skip it.

Priority targets, roughly in order of typical payoff:
1. **The actual business**, when identifiable: public profiles, product listings, signage, existing materials, reviews' recurring themes (as patterns, not quotes), how it presents itself. This reveals the real identity and existing visual cues the brief omits.
2. **The category's visual conventions**, so you know what the audience expects and what would make this business indistinguishable from its neighbors.
3. **Local competitive context**, when the business competes in a visible local setting.
4. **The environment**, when it is specific (a market type, a street setting, a venue, an event, a marketplace listing, a package category) and lighting or competing visuals matter.
5. **Cultural references** the piece may draw on, when the business connects to a region, tradition, language register, or occasion.
6. **Product reality**, so illustrated or photographed products look like what the business actually sells.

Scale effort: a straightforward brief with a clear identity may need little or none; an obscure niche, an unfamiliar cultural context, or a high-visibility large format deserves more. Stop when the remaining uncertainty would not change a decision. As a working guide, a handful of targeted searches usually resolves a typical brief; continue beyond that only while each new search is still changing a decision, and stop at diminishing returns.

If search tools are unavailable, proceed from the brief and general knowledge, mark affected conclusions as provisional internally, and avoid design choices whose correctness depends on unverified specifics.

## 3. Evaluating evidence

- Prefer the business's own materials, authoritative references, and first-hand sources over aggregators and anonymous commentary.
- Check that a found business is actually the user's: matching name, location, and offering. A wrong match is worse than no research.
- Treat reviews and social posts as signals of customer perception, not as verified facts about the business.
- Separate **fact** (verifiable), **interpretation** (your reading), **assumption** (untested), and **creative choice**. Carry the tags forward.
- Use competitors and references to understand conventions and find gaps. Never reproduce a layout, mark, slogan, or distinctive treatment.
- Conflicting sources: prefer the more primary and more recent one; if unresolved and consequential, treat as uncertain.

## 4. Understanding the brand

Look beneath the description for: values, personality, character, core message, positioning, credibility sources, aspirations, and the relationship the business has (or wants) with customers. Questions to ask:

- What does the business actually do better, differently, or more personally than its alternatives?
- What is the source of its credibility: craft, origin, price, convenience, speed, heritage, a person, a process, a community?
- How does it want to be perceived, and how is it likely perceived now? Where is the gap?
- Does it speak formally or intimately, modestly or boldly? What does its existing language reveal?
- Is it established with an identity to protect, or new with an identity to be built?

Distinguish established brand facts from plausible interpretations. A brand narrative is only as rich as the evidence. When evidence is thin, a restrained, honest identity serves better than an invented story.

## 5. Finding meaningful differentiation

Search for the strongest defensible differentiator. Candidates come from: product specifics, process, ingredients or materials actually used, origin, scale and intimacy, service behavior, price logic, location, founder or family, name, a signature item, a ritual around consumption or use.

Evaluate each candidate on:
- **Truth**: can the business honestly claim it?
- **Relevance**: does the audience care?
- **Distinctiveness**: would neighbors and competitors struggle to claim it?
- **Visualizability**: can it become a recognizable visual or verbal identifier?

Reject differentiators that fit dozens of competitors equally well. If none is strong, differentiate through execution: a sharper point of view, a more disciplined system, a distinctive hero, or a better-resolved mark, and record that this is the strategy.

## 6. Understanding the environment

Reconstruct the real encounter with the piece:
- Where does it physically sit, and what surrounds it? Competing signage, clutter, glare, shade, motion, crowd density, indoor atmosphere.
- What ambient light will it live under, and what does that do to color and contrast?
- How is it viewed: static or while moving, deliberate or incidental, at arm's length or from across a street, once or repeatedly?
- What are the neighbors communicating, and what visual behavior would make this piece stand out *appropriately* rather than merely louder?
- What cultural context does the setting carry: community expectations, occasion, local reading habits?

Distinguish an environment that rewards boldness from one that rewards calm. Contrast with the surroundings is a tool, not a rule.

## 7. Understanding the audience

Build a grounded picture, not a demographic caricature.

- **Context of encounter**: what are they doing and thinking when they meet the piece?
- **Motivation and need**: what problem or desire brings them to this category?
- **Attention triggers**: what would make them notice this piece among others?
- **Relevance**: what makes the offer feel meant for them?
- **Confidence**: what makes them trust a small business they may not know?
- **Action**: what is the easiest next step for them, and does the piece make it obvious?
- **Cultural and emotional associations**: language register, occasion, values, shared references.
- **Purchasing considerations**: price sensitivity, group decisions, habit, impulse, recommendation.

Do not assume that age, gender, or income determines taste. Do not state psychological claims you cannot support. Where the audience is plural, identify the primary audience for this piece and design for them, with the secondary audience not alienated.

## 8. Defining the communication problem

Write the piece's job in plain terms, internally:

- **Primary objective**: the one thing the piece must achieve.
- **Secondary objectives**: supporting aims that must not compete with the primary.
- **Desired audience response**: what the viewer should think, feel, and do.
- **Necessary information**: the minimum content that makes the response possible.
- **Desired brand perception**: how the business should be read afterward.
- **Chain**: how attention, comprehension, credibility, and action depend on each other for this piece. Identify which link is weakest in this situation.

Then list **tensions**: visibility versus sophistication; information density versus legibility; memorability versus commercial clarity; brand ambition versus the printing budget; user taste versus audience expectation. Do not resolve tensions yet; flag them for Phase 3 to resolve deliberately.

## 9. Cultural grounding in the Indonesian context

Principles, not formulas:

- Indonesia's cultures, languages, religions, and local commercial conventions vary widely by region and community. Do not treat "Indonesian" as a single aesthetic or audience.
- Regional or traditional visual references are legitimate only when the business genuinely connects to that heritage. Using them as generic "local flavor" is shallow and can be disrespectful. Prefer the specific and true over the broadly decorative.
- Symbols, colors, motifs, and figures can carry religious, ceremonial, or social meanings that differ between communities. When using any culturally loaded reference, verify its meaning and appropriateness, and omit it if uncertain.
- Language register matters: formality, regional words, and colloquialisms signal who the business is talking to. Preserve the user's register on the piece.
- Local commercial conventions affect content: how prices, contact channels, ordering methods, payment acceptance, and trust marks are typically presented. Respect what the user supplies; do not add conventions they did not provide.
- Practical contexts are often modest in budget and fast in consumption. A design should look credible and considered at the user's actual production level, not aspire to a production it cannot afford.

## 10. Converting analysis into design implications

Produce a compact list of implications, each of the form: *because [finding], the design must / should / must avoid [consequence]*. Each implication carries a provenance tag. Typical categories:

- What must be seen first, second, and last.
- What the brand must feel like and must not feel like.
- What the audience will expect and what would surprise them productively.
- What the environment demands in contrast, scale, and simplicity.
- Which differentiator is the strongest candidate to carry the identity.
- Which constraints (budget, material, distance) cap the available complexity.

If an implication cannot be traced to a finding, it is a creative choice and belongs in Phase 3, not here.

## 11. Failure modes

| Failure | Detection | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Research for its own sake | Findings that change no decision | Stop; keep only what drives an implication |
| Narrative inflation | Brand story richer than the evidence | Cut to what is supported |
| Stereotype reliance | Audience or industry traits asserted without grounding | Replace with the specific encounter model |
| Generic positioning | Differentiator fits many competitors | Re-investigate or differentiate via execution |
| Copying competitors | Conclusions of the form "do what they do" | Extract the convention, then choose how to depart or deliberately align |
| Analysis paralysis | No communication problem statement after extended work | Write the problem now; refine in Phase 3 |
| Shallow cultural use | Culture treated as decoration | Tie any cultural reference to a real business connection or drop it |

## 12. Completion criteria and hand-off

Phase 2 is complete when:
- The communication problem (objective, response, necessary information, perception, weakest link) is stated.
- Tensions are listed.
- At least one defensible differentiation strategy is identified, or the execution-led alternative is chosen.
- The environment and audience encounter are described concretely enough to constrain design.
- Design implications are listed, tagged, and traceable to findings or the brief.

Hand-off to Phase 3: Communication Problem, tension list, differentiation strategy, environment and audience profile, tagged design implications.
