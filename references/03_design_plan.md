# Phase 3: Design Plan

## Phase card

**Principles in force**: 2 Structured exploration: apparent option named, three candidates, one from beyond the apparent, anti-anchoring evaluation. 4 Contextual specificity: swap, category, and context-swap tests. 5 Functional creativity: every novel idea does a job. 6 Intentional restraint: set the space intent now, before elements accumulate. 7 Internal consistency. 10 Effort follows impact: this phase fixes the design's identity.

**Inputs**: Communication Problem, Environment Profile, Visual source inventory, differentiation strategy, implications.

**Required decisions**
1. Brand design (enduring) separated from print-piece theme (this piece), with the impression expressed through the environment.
2. Core idea, from at least three candidates.
3. Style direction, from at least three candidates.
4. Hero or deliberate absence of one, from at least three candidates.
5. Governing principles, including the space intent and the mark's role.

**Outputs**: Design Plan; the candidates, strengths, weaknesses, and reasons for the decision summary.

**Before leaving**: re-read the principles above; confirm none of the three decisions was won by being the apparent option without a stated decisive criterion.

## 1. Purpose and scope

Synthesize the analysis into one coherent, strategically justified creative direction. This is where the design's identity is decided. The plan must integrate findings into a single idea rather than assigning a style to each finding independently. It should guide execution firmly without prescribing every element, because Phases 4 and 5 still have decisions to make.

## 2. Brand design versus print-piece theme

**Brand design** is the enduring visual expression of the business: how its character, values, positioning, and message are made visible. It should survive changes of format and medium. For an established business it is mostly *discovered and respected*; for a new one it is *proposed*, with restraint proportionate to the evidence. Define what must remain recognizable if the piece changes: the personality, the tonal register, the underlying visual logic, the distinctive cues.

**Print-piece theme** is the creative concept for this specific piece, built on the brand design but shaped by the medium, objective, audience, content, and environment. It may change between pieces for the same brand; the brand design should not.

Keep them separate in your thinking. If the theme requires contradicting the brand design, either the theme is wrong or the brand design needs a deliberate, justified evolution the user has approved.
**Expression follows the environment.** Hold the brand's impression constant and let the Environment Profile decide how it is expressed in this piece. The theme is where that translation happens: the same brand may be expressed through restraint in one landscape and through density or contrast in another, without the identity changing.

## 3. The core idea

The **core idea** is the unifying concept from which everything else follows. Test it by whether it is:

- **Specific**: it could only belong to this business and this piece.
- **Visual**: it can be seen, not just described as a feeling.
- **Generative**: it implies decisions about hero, style, typography, color, and layout, rather than merely labeling a mood.
- **Truthful**: it does not claim what the business is not.
- **Functional**: it advances the objective, not just the aesthetics.

A mood word is not a core idea. A product category is not a core idea. A core idea relates something true about the business to something the audience recognizes, in a way that suggests a visual structure.

## 4. Generating genuinely different directions

This applies to each consequential Phase 3 decision, taken in this order so each informs the next: the **core idea**, the **style synthesis**, and the **main visual concept** (hero). Follow the exploration protocol in `SKILL.md`.

- **At least three candidates per decision, at most one of them the apparent option.** Name the apparent option first as the benchmark (see the exploration protocol in `SKILL.md`). Candidates must differ on a structural axis, not in color or ornament, and all are written before any is evaluated.
- **Different sources, including beyond the apparent.** Draw candidates from different origins: the product's tangible truth, the customer's ritual or moment of use, the physical environment and its landscape, the strongest differentiator, a tension in the category, the name or origin, the material or process of the print piece itself, and the Visual source inventory from Phase 2. At least one candidate comes from the inventory's deeper specifics, and at least one from the Environment Profile's departure dimensions.
- **Burden of proof on the apparent.** The default association of the category, the culture, or the user's first-stated style may stay in the field, but it wins only by naming the criterion on which the less obvious candidates fell short. Being familiar, expected, or fitting is not such a criterion.
- **Fake variety check.** If two candidates would look alike to a viewer in the real setting, replace one.
- **Working notes only.** Each candidate is a few lines: what it is, what carries it, how it would look and behave in the setting.

## 5. Evaluating and selecting

Judge every candidate for each decision against the whole brief, weighted by the Communication Problem.

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Objective fit | Does it strengthen the weakest link in the attention, comprehension, credibility, action chain? |
| Brand fit | Is it true to the brand design and the differentiation strategy? |
| Audience fit | Will this audience read it correctly? |
| Environmental fit and contrast | Is it appropriate to the place, and does it depart from the surrounding landscape on an open dimension? |
| Distinctiveness | Would it stand apart from the category's default look? |
| Content capacity | Can it carry the mandatory content with the space and hierarchy the reading stages need? |
| Feasibility | Can an image model render it reliably, and can the user's printing hold it? |
| Coherence | Does it generate a unified system rather than a patchwork? |

For each candidate record concrete strengths, weaknesses, and trade-offs. Then engineer the strongest solution:

- **Select** a candidate outright when it clearly wins.
- **Transplant** a specific strength from another candidate into the winner when it fixes a named weakness.
- **Combine** candidates only under one logic. State the result as a single idea; if it needs "and also," it is a compromise, so prefer the stronger candidate.
- **Repair** the winner's weakness directly when the fix does not alter its identity.
- **Rebuild**: when all candidates fail the same criterion, construct a new one from what the comparison revealed.

Challenge the result: what assumption is it betting on, and what happens if that assumption is wrong? Record the choice, the decisive reason, what was kept from the others, and why the rejected directions lose, so execution does not drift back to them.

## 6. General style direction

The style direction is the precise aesthetic approach that expresses the core idea. Define it across dimensions rather than with a single label:

- **Visual language**: how form is constructed and how far it is abstracted.
- **Artistic and historical influences**: specific lineages, movements, craft traditions, or print cultures that inform it, named precisely.
- **Compositional behavior**: how stable or dynamic, balanced or tensioned, dense or spacious, ordered or flowing the arrangement is.
- **Material character**: the physical impression of the surface and printing process.
- **Image-making technique**: how illustrations or imagery are made and rendered, including line, shading, edge treatment, and level of finish.
- **Typographic expression**: the personality and construction of the lettering and how it behaves in the layout.
- **Light and atmosphere**, where the style involves them.

Use established, specific vocabulary whose meaning is shared by designers and by image models. Prefer a precise two- or three-dimension synthesis to an inflated string of adjectives. If you cannot say what an illustrator or printer would do differently because of a style term, the term is decoration; replace it with a concrete description.

Creativity comes from synthesis driven by the brief's conditions, not from adding unusual elements. Check that the chosen style is not simply the default association of the industry.
The style direction is a consequential decision and goes through the exploration protocol. Test the winner for obviousness: if the category's customers would predict it before reading the brief, it needs a reason strong enough to justify the lack of surprise, or a stronger candidate.

## 7. Main visual concept

Decide what visually represents the brand and carries the piece. Consider the full range: a symbolic mark, an object or product, a person or group, a character or mascot, an environment, an illustration, a photographic scene, abstract form, or typography as the image.

Decisions to make:
- **Hero or none**: does the piece need one dominant visual, several coordinated visuals, or a restrained typographic and structural treatment?
- **Hero type and why**: what does it communicate that the others cannot? Is it recognizable at the viewing distance? Does it truthfully represent what is sold?
- **Relationship to the mark**: does the hero support, echo, or compete with the logo mark?
- **Reproducibility**: can the model render the hero consistently? Complex scenes with many subjects, intricate hands, or large crowds are high-risk; prefer simpler, bolder subjects when reliability matters.

If the hero is a product, its depiction must match what the business really offers. If it involves people, avoid depicting real identifiable individuals and avoid stereotyped or tokenistic representation.
The hero is also a consequential decision: generate at least three structurally different candidates (different hero types, or none), evaluate them as in section 5, and engineer the result. Check the winner against the viewing stages: it must register at the stage where it is needed, and it must leave the space the hierarchy requires. Treat the apparent hero (the emblem or product image everyone would reach for) as the benchmark and require a candidate that represents the business through a lesser-seen but true element from the Visual source inventory.

## 8. Governing design principles

State the principles that will govern Phases 4 and 5. They are decisions about character, not a list of preferences:

- **Composition character**: calm or energetic, centered or asymmetric, contained or bleeding.
- **Visual energy and rhythm**: where emphasis and rest occur, how the eye moves.
- **Information density**: how much the piece carries, and how that is made digestible.
- **Emotional tone**: warm, precise, festive, authoritative, intimate, and the intensity.
- **Commercial positioning**: where the piece sits on the spectrum from accessible to premium, and what that implies about finish, restraint, and ornament.
- **Dominant and supporting relationships**: what leads, what accompanies, what recedes.
- **Boundaries**: what the design must never become, drawn from the rejected directions and the AI-slop diagnostics in `quality_control.md`.
- **Space intent and mark role**: the share of the format that must stay quiet and what that space does (isolating the glance content, framing the hero, separating tiers, setting the positioning), derived from the medium's size, the viewing distance, the content volume, and the positioning. Larger media raise the space intent; they do not raise the number of elements. Also whether the logo mark leads, anchors, or signs off. Phases 4 and 5 are bound by this intent.

Keep principles short and mutually consistent. Each should be actionable in Phase 4 or 5.

## 9. Stress tests

Apply before accepting the plan:

- **Swap test**: replace the business with a competitor in the same category. If the plan still works unchanged, it is generic. Revisit the core idea.
- **Category test**: remove the product from the description. Does the identity still feel like this brand? If it feels like the industry instead, it is stereotyped.
- **Distance and attention test**: imagine the piece in the environment from Phase 2. Is the plan's first impression strong enough there?
- **Contradiction scan**: do the brand design, theme, style, hero, and principles agree? Do any tensions from Phase 2 remain unresolved?
- **Ambition check**: is the plan more elaborate than the content, budget, or format can support?
- **Context-swap test**: place the same design in a different setting. If it would be equally right there, it was not shaped by this environment; revisit the expression.
- **Apparent-choice test**: would a stranger predict the hero and style from the category and place alone? If yes, the plan needs a stated decisive reason for the apparent choice, or a stronger candidate.

## 10. Failure modes

| Failure | Detection | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Mood-board plan | Adjectives instead of decisions | Replace each with a structural choice |
| Style transplant | Chosen style unconnected to the business | Re-derive from the core idea |
| First-idea lock-in | No alternative was genuinely considered | Generate a structurally different direction |
| Fake variety | Alternatives differ only superficially | Differ on structural axes |
| Overwritten brand | Theme erases established identity | Restore brand design priority |
| Unrenderable hero | Hero depends on complex rendering | Simplify or reframe |
| Over-prescription | Plan dictates every placement and size | Leave execution decisions to Phases 4–5 |
| Decorative cultural borrowing | Heritage used without a real connection | Tie to a genuine link or remove |
| Single-candidate decision | A consequential decision lacks three distinct candidates | Generate and compare them |
| Environment as afterthought | Direction justified by category and brand only | Re-derive using the departure and fit dimensions |
| Style by default | The obvious aesthetic won without being challenged | Name it as a candidate and make it compete |
| Apparent option adopted unchallenged | The winner is the first idea or the category default, with no decisive criterion named | Run the challenge step; name the runner-up |
| Anchored on the first candidate | Evaluation followed generation order and the first candidate won by default | Re-evaluate from the last candidate; remove the leader and re-compare |
| Space intent missing | Plan states no share of quiet space | Derive it from medium size, viewing distance, content volume, positioning |

## 11. Completion criteria and hand-off

Complete when the plan contains: brand design definition; print-piece theme; core idea; style direction across relevant dimensions; main visual concept with the hero decision; governing principles and boundaries; and a record of why alternatives were rejected. It must pass the stress tests.

Hand-off to Phase 4: the Design Plan plus the unresolved questions it deliberately leaves to element design: the logo mark construction, the color logic, and the typographic voice.
Each consequential decision shows at least three distinct candidates, an evaluation, and an engineered result. The plan states how the brand impression is expressed in this environment.
Record for the decision summary the candidates compared for the core idea, style direction, and hero, with their key strengths and weaknesses and the reason the chosen direction won.
