## 1. Process

1. Separate brand design from print-piece theme; decide how the brand's impression is expressed in this environment (A, B).
2. Survey design territory (C).
3. Decide the core idea by the protocol (D).
4. Decide the style direction by the protocol, resolving its seven dimensions (E).
5. Decide the hero, or its deliberate absence, by the protocol (F).
6. Decide each governing principle, including space intent and mark role, by option comparison; resolve the Phase 2 tensions (G).
7. Evaluate the assembled plan across the full context (H); run the stress tests (I).
8. Record candidates, strengths, weaknesses, decisive criteria, rejected directions; pass the gate; guided mode: print the Phase 3 entry, ask for confirmation, stop (Phase 4 begins on the go-ahead); auto mode: print nothing and continue.

The three protocol decisions are sequential: the winning core idea becomes a criterion for style; the winning style a criterion for the hero. Write no style candidates before the core idea is chosen. Check each candidate's renderability by an image model (the prompt specialist's standing duty in Phases 3 to 5).

## 2. Detailed guidance

### A. Brand design

**Analyze:** how identity, personality, values, positioning, and core message are expressed visually; the enduring identity that must stay recognizable across formats.

**Consider:** for an established brand, what is discovered and must be respected (fixed assets bind it); for a new brand, what the evidence supports, proposed with restraint proportional to it.

**Evaluate** (at least two options for how the identity is expressed, each with strengths and weaknesses across the `protocol.md` section 3 parameter set): fit with the brand reading and positioning; durability across formats; distance from neighbors in the Environment Profile.

**Record:** the brand design, stated separately from the theme so the theme can vary without damaging it.

### B. Print-piece theme

**Analyze:** the medium, objectives, audience, content, and environment the theme must serve.

**Consider:** the brand's impression stays constant while the Environment Profile decides how it is expressed here; whether the theme contradicts the brand design (if so, the theme is wrong or the brand design needs a deliberate, approved evolution).

**Evaluate** (at least two theme options against the full parameter set): does it translate the brand into an idea suited to this piece? Does it leave room for a specific core idea?

**Record:** the theme and the contradiction check.

### C. Design territory survey

**Purpose.** Shallow options come from drawing only on what the model already associates with the category. The survey widens the field before any candidate is written.

**Survey distinct territories across source families (Full: at least five territories from three families; Focused: three from two):**
- historical and movement lineages in design, illustration, lettering, and print;
- regional and vernacular visual traditions connected to the business's place or community;
- craft, material, and production traditions (how things are made, printed, painted, carved, woven, stamped);
- contemporary practice in other categories and regions (cross-category, not competitor copying);
- the business's own world (Visual source inventory and Environment Profile).

**For each territory, study and record**
- Defining traits: depiction mode, image-making technique, line and form behavior, material and surface character, light and atmosphere, chromatic logic, typographic stance, compositional behavior.
- Lineage and the conditions it arose from, so it is used with understanding, not as a surface.
- Where it is true to this business, and where it would be imported whole.
- Behavior at the glance and approach stages in the real setting; fit with the departure and fit dimensions.
- Print and renderability demands.

**How to research**
- With search tools, look at real precedents (visual references, design histories, local material culture) before judging; without, reason from knowledge and mark the survey provisional.
- Study precedents for principles; never copy a competitor or an existing design.
- Verify the meaning and standing of any culturally loaded territory; omit it if uncertain.

**Record:** the survey as a short table: territory, source family, defining traits, fit, risk. Candidates in D to F draw on it; at least one candidate per decision must.

### D. The core idea

**Analyze.** A core idea relates something true about the business to something the audience recognizes, in a way that suggests a visual structure. A mood word or product category is not a core idea.

**Candidate card fields (core idea)**
1. The idea in one sentence.
2. Source: survey territory, inventory item, departure dimension, weakest link, differentiator, or name or origin.
3. The true thing and the audience recognition it relates.
4. The visual structure it suggests.
5. Behavior in the setting at each viewing stage.
6. Risks (incl. renderability and content capacity).
7. Compatibility with each quality requirement (supports, strains, neutral).

**Evaluate**
- Name the apparent core idea first as the benchmark.
- Candidates differ on a structural axis (what carries the identity, what the viewer does first, how information is structured, register, image-making method), not on color or ornament.
- Test every candidate on five properties: **specific** (belongs to this business), **visual** (suggests something seen), **generative** (theme, hero, style, and layout can follow), **truthful** (the business can honestly claim it), **functional** (helps the piece do its job in its setting and reads at the glance stage without being decoded).
- Run the context matrix, the interaction check against brand design and theme, and the originality tests (`protocol.md` section 3).

**Record:** the decision record (`protocol.md` section 2).

### E. Style direction

**Analyze.** A style direction is one synthesis resolved across seven dimensions, each decided in specialist terms:
1. **Visual language:** form and line behavior, level of abstraction, geometry or organic quality.
2. **Artistic and historical influences:** named by lineage and the traits borrowed, not by mood.
3. **Compositional behavior:** axis, alignment, grid logic, figure-ground relation, plane behavior (single plane or layered, and the layer cues used), tension, rhythm.
4. **Material character:** surface, texture, and the physical process the image evokes.
5. **Image-making technique:** how the image is produced (drawn, painted, photographic, constructed, printed) and its rendering logic.
6. **Typographic expression:** classification and stance, stroke contrast, proportion, spacing character.
7. **Light, atmosphere, and chromatic stance:** direction and quality of light, tonal key, value structure, temperature and harmony logic (the palette is decided in Phase 4 and must realize this stance).

**Candidate card fields (style).** A complete synthesis: one entry per dimension, plus the survey territories drawn on, the core idea realized, and compatibility with each quality requirement. A list of mood adjectives is not a candidate.

**Evaluate**
- For each dimension of the leading candidates, compare at least two options and record how each interacts with the others (repair a conflicting dimension before choosing the style).
- Run the context matrix, the interaction check against the core idea, and the originality tests; test the winner for obviousness.
- Creativity comes from synthesis of the brief's conditions, not arbitrary additions; avoid familiar combinations unless evaluated and justified.

**Record:** the decision record and the seven resolved dimensions.

### F. Main visual concept (hero)

**Analyze**
- Options: symbolic marks, objects, products, in-use scenes, places, figures (supplied or wanted), illustrations, photography, abstract forms, typography-led concepts, an equal set of images, and restraint with no hero.
- Choose a hero only when one subject deserves primary attention and a strong, renderable depiction exists. When several messages carry equal weight, or no suitable depiction exists, the answer is no hero or a small equal set, never a forced focal image.
- A figure (character or person) leads as hero only when supplied or wanted by the user, then as a designed character (Phase 4), never a stock figure or a likeness of a real person. Depiction mode (photographic, rendered, illustrated) follows what must be true: where the viewer must see exactly what is sold, a mode that stylizes it away is ineligible; where the subject is abstract or cannot be photographed, a drawn mode is eligible.

**Candidate card fields (hero)**
1. What it is, down to the true specifics that make it this business's own (form, material, construction, arrangement), with depiction mode and technique.
2. What it truthfully represents (a product hero matches what the business actually offers).
3. The viewing stage at which it registers, and how.
4. Relation to the mark: supports or competes.
5. Renderability and reliability (avoid real identifiable people; avoid dependence on complex hands, crowds, or fine faces unless reliability allows).
6. Space cost against the space intent.
7. Compatibility with each quality requirement.
8. Hosting capacity: what its form and visible parts can carry (text arcing over a dome, callouts to named parts, an overlapping tag), the scale and edge behavior at which it carries the message (isolated from its ground, or running to the trim), and which parts may sit in front of or behind text.

**Evaluate**
- Run the context matrix, the interaction check against core idea and style, and the originality tests.
- The hero's scale, crop, and edge behavior must express the message, not merely fit the space: a hero too small or too contained for its message fails even if attractive.
- For a standalone logo request the mark is the hero by definition; the protocol still runs for core idea and style, and the mark is developed in Phase 4.

**Record:** the decision record.

### G. Governing principles

**Purpose.** Each governing principle is a decision with alternatives, not a default. At Focused depth only space intent, imagery stance, integration stance, and mark role are compared; the rest are decided directly with a one-line basis. For each, compare at least two options against the core idea, style, hero, and parameter set, with one line of strength and weakness each, then select.

**Decide**
- **Character and tone.** The personality and register the piece speaks in, as checkable qualities (formality, warmth, assertiveness, directness). Derive it from brand design, audience, core idea, and setting, comparing at least two ways to voice the brand impression here. It governs typographic stance, headline language, and the hero's manner. A tone that signals the opposite of a stated quality is eliminated.
- **Commercial positioning.** Where the offer sits on the value spectrum, economical to premium, and the cues that signal it (restraint or abundance, finish, scale, space, price visibility). Derive it from what the business truthfully offers, its neighbors in the Environment Profile, and audience expectations. It is read from many small cues together, so one contradicting cue undoes the rest. It feeds space intent, density, and tone, and must be honest to the offer.
- **Energy and rhythm.** The activity level (calm, steady, charged) and how the eye is paced: where attention accelerates, where it rests. Derive it from the core idea, the style's compositional behavior, the setting's competing stimuli, and the viewing stages, comparing options that differ in intensity and distribution. The glance stage needs a clear first beat; uniform loudness or quiet is no rhythm. Stay proportionate to density and space intent, consistent with tone.
- **Density.** How much the eye must take in per unit of area: element count, information grain, packing. Derive it from the content inventory, format size, viewing distance, and positioning; fix it as a stated level, bounded by space intent and weighed against the hero's need for dominance. Content beyond the level is triaged now (cut, merge, demote), not shrunk. Higher density demands stricter grouping and hierarchy, never more decoration.
- **Dominant and supporting relationships.** The ranking of what leads, supports, and recedes across hero, identity unit, required information, supporting visuals, and ornament, each supporter tied to what it serves, with the contrast between ranks stated. Derive it from the Communication Problem, hero decision, and mark role, comparing at least two orderings. The glance stage has one clear leader, each rank differs by more than one means, and no supporter outranks what it serves.
- **Point of tension.** Whether the plan holds one deliberate break from its own otherwise coherent system (in scale, contrast, placement, or concept), and which element carries it. Decide after hero and style, from what the core idea makes memorable, compared with having none; every other decision then supports it. Limits: at most one; carried only by an element allowed to lead (hero, identity unit, designed hook or transaction); compatible with every quality requirement; it must lower the effort of reading or raise delight at the glance stage, never add decoding. None is the answer when the core idea, hero, or departure dimension already supplies the unexpected, or the style and qualities call for uniform calm. Chosen, never a default addition.
- **Boundaries.** What the design must never become, named as qualities and treatments, drawn first from the apparent options and the neighbors' look already recorded (baselines to avoid, the image model's included), then from the brief, production, and culture. Each is realized later as a decided opposite trait, not a bare exclusion. A whole imagery family is excluded only for a stated reason (brief, style, production, culture), never by blanket default.
- **Space intent.** The share of the format that must stay quiet, and what that space does (separates glance content, gives the eye rest, signals positioning). Derive it from format size, viewing distance, content volume, and positioning. Larger media raise the space intent or the strength of the main idea, not the number of elements. Test with a rough capacity estimate: mandatory content at its legibility minimums must fit the non-quiet share; if not, lower the space intent or plan triage now, not in Phase 5.
- **Integration stance.** Whether content about the hero is composed with it (integrated or attached) or kept in separate zones, decided by what the content refers to and the hero's hosting capacity (Phase 5 relationship map). Default: couple content that labels, explains, or prices the hero. Exception: a hero that carries the message alone, or one coupling would dilute (competing for its edge, hiding its form, shrinking it); there separate zones with isolation is stronger, because the hero's dominance is the asset and each coupling spends it. Decide from the core idea, the hero's hosting capacity, and the content inventory; theme, style, and audience set how far coupling goes.
- **Layering stance.** How many planes the piece uses (one, or up to three), what each carries (hero, information, supporters, base field), and how strongly the order is expressed. Plane order ranks content (forward reads as more important), integrates content with the hero through overlap, and adds richness. Derive it from the style's plane behavior, the hero's hosting capacity, the integration stance, space intent, and viewing distance, comparing at least two options, a single plane among them. Choose more planes only for a named gain (rank, integration, mood, surface richness). A flat style keeps one picture plane and gains richness from tonal steps, quiet surface detail, and overlap of flat shapes, never illusionistic volume. Limits: required information stays on a plane where it is legible and unoccluded; layering adds no element beyond the space intent; cues must survive the viewing distance and production; a plane's added prominence counts against the weight ceiling.
- **Imagery stance and supporting-visual budget.** Which supporter families are open (icons and symbols, subject, in-use, place, or spot imagery, figures, image treatments, pattern or texture, borders and frames, dividers, connectors and direction cues, shape blocks, lettering-as-graphic), what share of the format they may occupy, and the form language they share. Derive it from the style, content volume, the visual nature of what is offered, and the quality requirements. An open family is a permission, never an obligation: Phase 4 uses it only for a deficit it answers. The stance also fixes the ceiling: supporters rank below the hero, the identity unit, and the required information they serve. Heavy text raises the need for visual relief, never licenses clutter.
- **Figure stance (only when a figure is supplied or wanted).** Which roles the figure plays (identify as part of the mark, lead as hero, demonstrate the offer in use, guide attention, carry mood), chosen against the core idea, hero, and mark role. Each role answers a need no other element answers, one figure serves them all, and the figure stays subordinate to required information except as hero or mark. A figure the user did not mention is offered once as a suggestion where a real deficit calls for it, and never enters the plan unconfirmed.
- **Quality carriers.** Assign each quality requirement's carriers to plan decisions (color and value key, style, hero, density, tone). A requirement with no carrier is a gap in the plan.
- **Mark role.** Whether the identity unit leads the hierarchy, anchors it, or signs off, chosen against the Communication Problem and the hero. It fixes how much weight Phases 4 and 5 give the identity unit.

**Resolve the Phase 2 tensions** one by one: state which side leads and what the other side keeps.

### H. Evaluating the assembled plan across the full context

**Check** as one system, not decision by decision:
- Do brand design, theme, core idea, style, hero, and governing principles reinforce one another, or does any pair conflict?
- Does the plan, taken whole, answer the weakest link in the Environment Profile's real setting?
- Does it fit content volume, space intent, medium, budget, and renderability together, not separately?
- Which weaknesses were accepted, and do accepted costs compound?

Return to the decision responsible for any conflict; cap at two revision cycles per issue.

### I. Stress tests

Run all eight and record each result:
1. **Swap test.** Substitute a competitor; if the plan still works unchanged, it is generic.
2. **Category test.** Remove the product; if the identity could belong to the industry, it is stereotyped.
3. **Apparent-choice test.** Would a stranger predict the hero and style from category and place alone?
4. **Context-swap test.** Would the plan be equally right in another setting? If so, it ignores the environment.
5. **Distance and attention test.** Does the first impression hold at the glance stage in the real setting?
6. **Contradiction scan.** Do all plan decisions agree?
7. **Ambition check.** Against content, budget, and format.
8. **Brief-quality test.** Read each stated quality against the plan: does it have a carrier at the glance stage, and does any decision signal its opposite?

## 3. Failure modes

| Failure | Detection | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Options from the model's first associations | No survey, or all territories from one source family | Run the survey across the depth's minimum of source families |
| Label candidates | A card has empty or adjectival fields | Fill every field in specialist terms, or discard |
| Single-variable evaluation | Option chosen on one parameter ("it looks premium") | Run the full context matrix and interaction check |
| Unmitigated weakness | A chosen direction has a weakness with no move and no accepted cost | Apply strength-weakness engineering |
| Mood-word core idea | The idea cannot generate a hero or layout | Re-source from the survey or inventory |
| Apparent option wins by default | No decisive criterion, or "it is fitting" as the reason | Name the criterion on which less obvious candidates fell short, or choose among them |
| Merely competent winner | The choice is safe and forgettable | Apply the distinctness test; develop the ambitious candidate |
| Style as adjective list | An illustrator could not act on it | Restate across the seven dimensions |
| Style stack by assignment | A different style assigned to each insight | Synthesize one style |
| Environment-blind plan | Passes the context-swap test unchanged | Re-derive with the departure dimension as a source |
| Hero that misrepresents | The hero is not what is sold | Replace or restrain |
| Forced hero | A focal image chosen though messages are equal or no renderable depiction exists | Choose no hero or an equal set |
| Unwanted or roleless figure | A figure the user neither supplied nor wanted, or with no role only it performs | Remove it, or offer it once as a suggestion |
| Governing principles defaulted | A principle stated with no compared options | Compare at least two options for each |
| Space intent missing or vague | No share, no job | Derive from format, distance, volume, positioning |
| Compromise instead of hybrid | The chosen idea needs "and also" | Choose one logic |
| Brand design and theme collapse | The theme alters the enduring identity without approval | Separate them again |
| Imported territory | A territory used as surface, lineage not understood | Study its conditions, or drop it |
| Borrowed design | A direction mirrors a competitor's or an existing design | Re-source from this business's world |
| Environment over brief | A departure or style choice signals the opposite of a stated quality | Re-run with quality compatibility as an elimination criterion |
| Blanket imagery exclusion | A whole family (photo, icon, illustration, pattern) ruled out with no stated reason | Reopen the family, or record the reason |
| Space intent not capacity-tested | Content cannot fit the non-quiet share at its minimums | Lower the space intent or plan triage now |

## 4. Completion criteria, summary print, and hand-off

Complete when the phase card's "Before leaving" check holds.

**Print for the decision summary (Design Plan entry, at the gate):** for core idea, style, and hero, each: the apparent option, candidates compared (one line each: name, key strength, key weakness), the selection with a one-sentence rationale including why it beat the runner-up, the decisive criterion, and what was combined or repaired. Add one line each for the governing principles that had real alternatives (space intent, imagery stance, and mark role always), plus one line on how each stated quality is carried. Mark any decision fixed by the user or evidence as such. Update the Design Log: survey, core idea, style, hero, space intent, mark role, open issues.

**Hand-off to Phase 4:** the Design Plan (core idea, style across seven dimensions, hero, governing principles incl. space intent and mark role), the survey, the rejected directions, plus the Environment Profile, Visual source inventory, and Phase 1's content, fixed assets, and constraints.
