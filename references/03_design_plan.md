## 1. Process

1. Separate brand design from print-piece theme; decide how the brand's impression is expressed in this environment (A, B).
2. Survey design territory (C).
3. Decide the core idea by the protocol (D).
4. Decide the style direction by the protocol, resolving its seven dimensions (E).
5. Decide the hero, or its deliberate absence, by the protocol (F).
6. Decide each governing principle, including space intent and mark role, by option comparison; resolve the Phase 2 tensions (G).
7. Evaluate the assembled plan across the full context (H) and run the stress tests (I).
8. Record candidates, strengths, weaknesses, decisive criteria, and rejected directions; pass the gate; print the entry.

The three protocol decisions are sequential: the winning core idea becomes a criterion for style; the winning style becomes a criterion for the hero. Do not write style candidates before the core idea is chosen. Check each candidate's renderability by an image model (the prompt specialist's standing duty in Phases 3 to 5).

## 2. Detailed guidance

### A. Brand design

**Analyze**
- How identity, personality, values, positioning, and core message are expressed visually.
- The enduring identity that must stay recognizable across formats.

**Consider**
- For an established brand: what is discovered and must be respected (fixed assets bind it).
- For a new brand: what the evidence supports, proposed with restraint proportional to that evidence.

**Evaluate** (at least two options for how the identity is expressed, each with strengths and weaknesses across the parameter set in SKILL.md 7.3)
- Fit with the brand reading and positioning.
- Durability across formats.
- Distance from neighbors in the Environment Profile.

**Record**
- The brand design, stated separately from the theme so the theme can vary without damaging it.

### B. Print-piece theme

**Analyze**
- The medium, objectives, audience, content, and environment the theme must serve.

**Consider**
- That the brand's impression stays constant and the Environment Profile decides how it is expressed here.
- Whether the theme contradicts the brand design; if so, either the theme is wrong or the brand design needs a deliberate, approved evolution.

**Evaluate** (at least two theme options against the full parameter set)
- Does it translate the brand into an idea suited to this piece?
- Does it leave room for the core idea to be specific?

**Record**
- The theme and the contradiction check.

### C. Design territory survey

**Purpose.** Shallow options come from drawing only on what the model already associates with the category. The survey widens the field before any candidate is written.

**Survey at least five distinct territories from at least three source families:**
- historical and movement lineages in design, illustration, lettering, and print;
- regional and vernacular visual traditions connected to the business's place or community;
- craft, material, and production traditions (how things are made, printed, painted, carved, woven, stamped);
- contemporary practice in other categories and regions (cross-category, not competitor copying);
- the business's own world (the Visual source inventory and the Environment Profile).

**For each territory, study and record**
- Its defining traits: depiction mode, image-making technique, line and form behavior, material and surface character, light and atmosphere, chromatic logic, typographic stance, compositional behavior.
- Its lineage and the conditions it arose from, so that it is used with understanding rather than as a surface.
- Where it is true to this business, and where it would be imported whole.
- Its behavior at the glance and approach stages in the real setting, and its fit with the departure and fit dimensions.
- Its print and renderability demands.

**How to research**
- When search tools are available, look at real precedents (visual references, design histories, local material culture) before judging; when they are not, reason from knowledge and mark the survey provisional.
- Study precedents to understand principles; never copy a competitor or an existing design.
- Verify the meaning and standing of any culturally loaded territory; omit it if uncertain.

**Record**
- The survey as a short table: territory, source family, defining traits, fit, risk. Candidates in D to F draw on it, and at least one candidate per decision must.

### D. The core idea

**Analyze**
- A core idea relates something true about the business to something the audience recognizes, in a way that suggests a visual structure. A mood word or product category is not a core idea.

**Candidate card fields (core idea)**
1. The idea in one sentence.
2. Source: survey territory, inventory item, departure dimension, weakest link, differentiator, or name or origin.
3. The true thing and the audience recognition it relates.
4. The visual structure it suggests.
5. Its behavior in the setting at each viewing stage.
6. Risks (including renderability and content capacity).

**Evaluate**
- Name the apparent core idea first as the benchmark.
- Candidates differ on a structural axis (what carries the identity, what the viewer does first, how information is structured, register, image-making method), not on color or ornament.
- Test every candidate on five properties: **specific** (belongs to this business), **visual** (suggests something seen), **generative** (theme, hero, style, and layout can follow), **truthful** (the business can honestly claim it), **functional** (helps the piece do its job in its setting).
- Run the context matrix, the interaction check against brand design and theme, and the originality tests (SKILL.md 7.3).

**Record**
- The decision record (SKILL.md 7.2).

### E. Style direction

**Analyze.** A style direction is one synthesis resolved across seven dimensions. Each dimension is decided in specialist terms:
1. **Visual language:** form and line behavior, level of abstraction, geometry or organic quality.
2. **Artistic and historical influences:** named by lineage and the traits borrowed, not by mood.
3. **Compositional behavior:** axis, alignment, grid logic, figure-ground relation, tension, rhythm.
4. **Material character:** surface, texture, and the physical process the image evokes.
5. **Image-making technique:** how the image is produced (drawn, painted, photographic, constructed, printed) and its rendering logic.
6. **Typographic expression:** classification and stance, stroke contrast, proportion, spacing character.
7. **Light, atmosphere, and chromatic stance:** direction and quality of light, tonal key, value structure, temperature and harmony logic (the palette itself is decided in Phase 4 and must realize this stance).

**Candidate card fields (style).** A complete synthesis: one entry per dimension, plus the survey territories it draws on and the core idea it realizes. A list of mood adjectives is not a candidate.

**Evaluate**
- For each dimension of the leading candidates, compare at least two options and record how each interacts with the others (a dimension that conflicts with another is repaired before the style is chosen).
- Run the context matrix, the interaction check against the core idea, and the originality tests; test the winner for obviousness.
- Creativity comes from synthesis of the brief's conditions, not arbitrary additions; avoid familiar combinations unless evaluated and justified.

**Record**
- The decision record and the seven resolved dimensions.

### F. Main visual concept (hero)

**Analyze**
- Options include symbolic marks, objects, products, people, mascots, environments, illustrations, photography, abstract forms, typography-led concepts, and restraint with no hero.

**Candidate card fields (hero)**
1. What it is, and its depiction mode and technique.
2. What it truthfully represents (a product hero matches what the business actually offers).
3. The viewing stage at which it registers, and how.
4. Its relation to the mark: supports or competes.
5. Renderability and reliability (avoid real identifiable people; avoid dependence on complex hands, crowds, or fine faces unless reliability allows).
6. Its space cost against the space intent.

**Evaluate**
- Run the context matrix, the interaction check against core idea and style, and the originality tests.
- For a standalone logo request the mark is the hero by definition; the protocol still runs for the core idea and style, and the mark is developed in Phase 4.

**Record**
- The decision record.

### G. Governing principles

**Purpose.** Each governing principle is a decision with alternatives, not a default. For each, compare at least two options against the core idea, style, hero, and the parameter set, with one line of strength and weakness each, then select.

**Decide**
- **Character and tone.**
- **Commercial positioning.**
- **Energy and rhythm.**
- **Density.**
- **Dominant and supporting relationships.**
- **Boundaries:** what the design must never become.
- **Space intent:** the share of the format that must stay quiet, and what that space does (separates glance content, gives the eye rest, signals positioning). Derive it from format size, viewing distance, content volume, and positioning. Larger media raise the space intent or the strength of the main idea; they do not raise the number of elements.
- **Mark role:** whether the identity unit leads the hierarchy, anchors it, or signs off, chosen against the Communication Problem and the hero. The role fixes how much weight Phases 4 and 5 give the identity unit.

**Resolve the Phase 2 tensions** one by one: state which side leads and what the other side is allowed to keep.

### H. Evaluating the assembled plan across the full context

**Check** (not decision by decision, but as one system)
- Do brand design, theme, core idea, style, hero, and governing principles reinforce one another, or does any pair conflict?
- Does the plan, taken whole, answer the weakest link in the Environment Profile's real setting?
- Does the plan fit the content volume, space intent, medium, budget, and renderability together, not separately?
- Which weaknesses were accepted, and do accepted costs compound?

Return to the decision responsible for any conflict; cap at two revision cycles per issue.

### I. Stress tests

Run all seven and record the result of each:
1. **Swap test.** Substitute a competitor; if the plan still works unchanged, it is generic.
2. **Category test.** Remove the product; if the identity could belong to the industry, it is stereotyped.
3. **Apparent-choice test.** Would a stranger predict the hero and style from category and place alone?
4. **Context-swap test.** Would the plan be equally right in another setting? If so, it ignores the environment.
5. **Distance and attention test.** Does the first impression hold at the glance stage in the real setting?
6. **Contradiction scan.** Do all plan decisions agree?
7. **Ambition check.** Against content, budget, and format.

## 3. Failure modes

| Failure | Detection | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Options from the model's first associations | No survey, or survey territories all from one source family | Run the survey across at least three source families |
| Label candidates | A candidate card has empty or adjectival fields | Fill every field in specialist terms, or discard |
| Single-variable evaluation | An option chosen on one parameter ("it looks premium") | Run the full context matrix and interaction check |
| Unmitigated weakness | A chosen direction has a weakness with no move and no accepted cost | Apply strength-weakness engineering |
| Mood-word core idea | The idea cannot generate a hero or layout | Re-source from the survey or inventory |
| Apparent option wins by default | No decisive criterion, or "it is fitting" as the reason | Name the criterion on which less obvious candidates fell short, or choose among them |
| Merely competent winner | The choice is safe and forgettable | Apply the distinctness test; develop the ambitious candidate |
| Style as adjective list | An illustrator could not act on it | Re-state across the seven dimensions |
| Style stack by assignment | A different style assigned to each insight | Synthesize one style |
| Environment-blind plan | Passes the context-swap test unchanged | Re-derive with the departure dimension as a source |
| Hero that misrepresents | The hero is not what is sold | Replace or restrain |
| Governing principles defaulted | A principle stated with no compared options | Compare at least two options for each |
| Space intent missing or vague | No share, no job | Derive from format, distance, volume, positioning |
| Compromise instead of hybrid | The chosen idea needs "and also" | Choose one logic |
| Brand design and theme collapse | The theme alters the enduring identity without approval | Separate them again |
| Imported territory | A survey territory used as surface, with no lineage understood | Study its conditions, or drop it |
| Borrowed design | A direction mirrors a competitor's or an existing design | Re-source from this business's world |

## 4. Completion criteria, summary print, and hand-off

Complete when the phase card's "Before leaving" check holds.

**Print for the decision summary (Design Plan entry, at the gate):** for the core idea, style, and hero, each: the apparent option, the candidates compared (one line each: name, key strength, key weakness), the selection with a one-sentence rationale including why it beat the runner-up, the decisive criterion, and what was combined or repaired. Add one line each for the governing principles that had real alternatives (space intent and mark role always). Mark any decision fixed by the user or evidence as such. Update the Design Log: survey, core idea, style, hero, space intent, mark role, open issues.

**Hand-off to Phase 4:** the Design Plan (core idea, style across seven dimensions, hero, governing principles including space intent and mark role), the survey, the rejected directions, plus the Environment Profile, Visual source inventory, and Phase 1's content, fixed assets, and constraints.
