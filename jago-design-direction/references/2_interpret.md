# Phase 2: interpret the brief

**Needs:** S. **Produces:** the **interpretation**, in two files read together: the understanding of the business and its **strategic core** with its **perception contract** (this file, §1–3); and the communication problem, appeal bar, raw material, prior read, and **mandates** for Brand, Theme, and Picture (`2_context.md`, §4–8). This phase is the foundation: every layer inherits its conclusions and is judged against them, so an error here travels everywhere. **Guided:** no stop (a fork, §3, excepted); its findings lead the first card message.
**Order:** as numbered. A finding changing no decision is dropped.

## 1. How to interpret
Think as brand strategist, creative director, and communication designer: ask what the facts *imply*, under the evidence discipline and tests of §3.
- **Basis:** every conclusion is tagged given, inferred (from which facts), researched, or **open** (unresolved: it carries the default taken and the risk if the default is wrong, and is flagged in the notes when it shaped the brief). Aspiration above evidence is downgraded; a small fact never becomes an invented identity.
- **What matters:** a dimension of the business matters in proportion to its power to change a design decision. It is **binding** where a wrong visual signal would contradict the offer, mislead the audience, or break trust, and worth exploring where the business can distinguish itself. Find them by asking what the customer must believe about this business after one look, and which cue would make them believe the opposite. Explore binding dimensions as deep as the evidence allows; touch the rest lightly or omit them. The business and the brief decide which dimensions exist; none is assumed.
- **Implicit signals:** the brief says more than it states: how the business describes itself (vocabulary and register), what it chose to show (a price, a specific), how it names its customers, the venue, channel, and scale, the connotations of its name, the motif or mood the user chose, what it leaves out. Read them as evidence, tagged inferred with the evidence cited.
- **Sources:** S; every link and file supplied, opened and read; and, with a search tool, at most five targeted lookups in this phase (the business itself, its audience's world, peers' identities and strong current work, `2_context.md` §5). Research is weighed, never copied, never overrides the user.
- A reading too thin to support a decision is a fork (§3) or is tagged provisional with the most probable reading.

## 2. The reads
A few sentences each, basis-tagged; omit what changes no decision.
1. **Business.** Identity and background (what it is, how it began, who stands behind it, how it behaves, scale); **value basis** (what the customer gets for what they pay, and why they choose it over the alternatives they weigh) and the price and status tier the offer truly lives in; positioning (its category, its stance against the alternatives, what it competes on and declines to); **distinctive truth**, its USP (valued, true, unclaimed by named peers); personality and values as shown by conduct; credibility held and claims never to make; the owner's aspiration and how far its standing can carry a departure.
2. **Audience.** The ideal buyer as a specific person in a specific moment, not a segment: who, in what circumstance, wanting and wary of what; what they believe about the category and this business; the real motive, often different from the stated one; what they respect and find beautiful; the cues saying "for me" and "not for me". Where the decider differs from the user or the delighted, read both: what each must find, at which tier. From demographics alone, derive situational and motivational implications, tagged inferred.
3. **Perception and message.** The shift sought (what the audience thinks and feels now → what the business wants); the value to be credited; **the message** in one sentence (what the glance viewer takes away and does next); the proof that makes it believable. It serves the objective and contradicts no quality.
4. **Purpose and response.** The job the design does in the business's life; the response and call to action; what stands between viewer and action. **Objective:** a user-stated priority fixes it; the weakest link (`2_context.md` §4) is chosen within it and never replaces it.
5. **Atmosphere.** The state change sought (arrives as …, leaves as …), grounded in the viewer's state at the encounter and the business's personality. Stated qualities stay binding at their intensity; the derived atmosphere deepens them, never calms or narrows them.
6. **Other considerations.** Culture, timing, occasion, sensitivities, legal and channel limits, and what the name and motifs mean to this audience locally (respect, humor, status, history): anything else that changes a decision.

## 3. The cross-read
A summary is not an interpretation. First **chain** each decisive fact: fact → what it means to this audience → what it must therefore look or feel like → the layer that decides it; a fact with no visual consequence is not yet interpreted. Then read the reads and the implicit signals against each other for what appears only in combination: a tension between who the business is and what the audience expects; a true detail the audience values that peers do not use; a link between seemingly unrelated facts (origin, behavior, the audience's moment, the occasion); a gap between aspiration and evidence.
**Signal audit.** For each signal the brief sends (§1), state what it implies about the offer's tier, quality, category, and audience, and compare it with the value basis. A conflict is never left as a "productive tension": it is resolved by naming what each side governs. **Subject versus register:** a name, motif, or mood the user chose governs the **subject** (what is depicted and what the idea is about); the proposition governs the **register** (what the finish, material richness, ornament, and polish signal about tier, quality, and category), unless the user stated the register. A conflict this cannot settle (including a stated register that contradicts the proposition), or that the evidence cannot settle, is a fork.
Produce the **strategic core**:
- **Design truth:** "[specific true thing], [productive tension]", the tension a contrast inside the business or the audience's moment, with the side that leads the visible register named; it must fail the **swap test** (a named peer makes it false). If nothing passes, say so: the identity rests on name, place, and audience moment, claims stay modest, and the notes invite one more detail.
- **Stance:** how the business carries itself toward this audience, as conduct, one sentence, no stacked adjectives.
- **Perception shift** and **message**, sharpened by the cross-read.
- **Perception contract:** for each binding dimension, the position the look must signal and the cues that would signal against it, stated as visible carriers (finish, material richness, ornament density, restraint, scale of space, lettering polish, imagery handling), never as adjectives alone. Craft is not register: excellent making can still contradict the contract.
**Five tests, per conclusion:** **Evidence** (basis stated) · **Swap** (false for a named peer or another customer) · **Obviousness** (not inferable from the category alone) · **Opposite** (its opposite does not fit the evidence equally) · **Use** (it changes a named decision at a named layer). Failing Opposite makes a **fork**: ask once, one short message of at most two questions, each offering two to four readings; if skipped, take the best-supported reading, tag it, flag it in the notes. Failing Obviousness or Use: deepen with the user's specifics or drop. **Stated differentiators:** every strength, specification, or reason to choose the user stated is used by the core or ranked below another truth with a stated reason; none is silently dropped.
