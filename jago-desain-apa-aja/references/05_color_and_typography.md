## 1. Process

1. Inventory fixed assets and reproduction limits.
2. Decide color (A) and type (B) as provisional constraints for the mark; both must realize Phase 3 style dimensions 6 and 7.
3. Develop the mark and lockup (`06_identity_unit_and_copy_treatment.md` C). If it exposes a conflict with color or type, adjust them within this phase.
4. Decide copy treatment by content role and provisional rank (`06_identity_unit_and_copy_treatment.md` B2); run the text-only baseline and choose supporters (`07_visual_supporters_and_figures.md` D); decide container jobs, shape language, and fill modes (`08_containers_and_ground.md` D2).
5. Decide background and unifying devices (`08_containers_and_ground.md` E).
6. Evaluate the system as a whole (`09_element_system_evaluation.md` F); gate.

## 2. Guidance

### A. Color

**Inputs:** Phase 3 style dimension 7 (chromatic stance), space budget (quiet share, role allocations), hero, and mark role; Phase 2 message (these set which items rank first or second for color distribution; final ranks are Phase 5's); fixed brand colors and reproduction limits (ink count, substrate, finish, or the display's equivalents); the surrounding color field and ambient light or display conditions.

**Options** differ in logic, not only hue: what color does here (organize, signal, separate, evoke, depart from the surroundings). Per option, record:
- roles (dominant, supporting, accent; number of hues and area proportion);
- harmony, value structure, chroma, temperature;
- contrast per encounter stage;
- behavior under reproduction limits and in the real light;
- verified cultural associations;
- quality compatibility.

Monochrome, dual-tone, restrained multicolor, and complex systems are all valid when justified.

**Value-key check (mandatory).** Compute the key from area proportions, counting the ground and every large band or block. Dark masses make a low-key piece however light the accents. Eliminate any option whose key contradicts a quality requirement; achieve departure through hue, chroma, or form within a compatible key. Describe colors to the model by named hues with qualifiers and roles; use hex only for supplied brand colors or where precision is essential.

**Color distribution.** Area proportion is not prominence. For each color role record its area share, its intensity (chroma, value contrast against its neighbors, temperature), and where it sits: a small saturated accent outweighs a large muted field. The strongest chroma and value contrast belong to the first-rank levers (the accent sits on what ranks first or second, never spread across many items). Large areas carry restrained chroma unless a saturated dominant field is the plan's departure, and then everything on it is calmed in intensity. No secondary or utility item takes a high-intensity color role, and pattern, ornament, and atmosphere fields take their colors from the palette at calm intensity so they enrich without competing with ranked items.

### B. Typography

Typography is part of the art direction: it carries the subject's personality and the piece's world as much as color or imagery do, and it is decided per role, never as one family with a ladder of sizes. Decide the typographic voice and the roles the content needs against the lettering of the surroundings and Phase 3 typographic expression (dimension 6).

- **Voice and character.** Name what the lettering says about this subject (personality and register, drawn from the lettering lineages and traditions in the territories and inventory, never the category's stock face), then give each role its construction: classification, stroke contrast, proportion, x-height, width, terminals, case, and posture. Weight, width, and proportion are chosen as instruments of the role, never left at regular.
- **Roles and pairing.** Roles come from the content (display, supporting, informational, numerals and prices, small print, and any promotional or action role). State the logic that relates them: what they share (a construction trait that makes them one family of voices) and what they contrast (the trait that tells them apart). Options differ in stance, not only in face. Expressive display lettering (drawn, modulated, or structurally customized) is a treatment of the display role (`06_identity_unit_and_copy_treatment.md` B2), not an extra family.
- **Texture, spacing, and orientation.** Per role decide letter-spacing, word spacing, line spacing, the density of the text mass (measure and number of lines a block carries), alignment (flush, centered, ragged side), and orientation (level by default; rotation or a vertical setting is a decision with a reason). Tracking and leading are part of a role's character: tight display, open small capitals, and relaxed informational text are choices, never defaults. These feed each group's space need (`11_space_plan_and_spatial_strategy.md`, B3 and B4).
- **Differentiation by purpose.**
  - Each role is styled to the success measure of its purpose, which comes from what the audience needs from the item, stated per role.
  - Roles present in the same text group must be told apart at a glance by at least one clear contrast other than scale (classification, weight, width, posture, case, spacing, color). Scale ranks roles; it does not by itself differentiate them. Headline, tagline, description, promotional message, supporting information, and call to action never inherit one treatment at different sizes.
  - Equivalent items share one style everywhere they appear, whatever their length or proximity to other text. Deliberate variation needs a difference in importance, role, emphasis, or purpose, recorded.
  - One family is acceptable only when weight, posture, case, width, or spacing contrast alone separates the roles. Otherwise pair two families at most, with variants, to stay within what the model renders reliably.
  - Keep roles few but never indistinguishable. Sizes are ratios between roles that follow encounter stages.
  - The wordmark's lettering must differ from the informational type in construction, weight, or treatment (`06_identity_unit_and_copy_treatment.md` C).
  - Dense text raises rendering risk: plan structure (grouping, rows, fewer styles).
  - Describe type by characteristics; name a font only for a stated brand requirement.
- **Readability and expression.** Each role is legible at the physical size and viewing distance of its stage and in the real light (the Phase 2 tier floors). Expression never costs legibility: the more expressive the voice, the fewer the roles that carry it, and the informational roles stay easy to read.

**Letterform proportions.** Each role's width class, posture, and baseline are decided and named, never left to the model.
- Informational and plain-set text keeps natural proportions and a level baseline.
- Width or posture contrast separates roles only where the role's purpose gains from it (voice, emphasis), never to make text fit.
- Fitting is done by container size, line breaks, tier size, or the fit ladder (`terms_design_system.md`, Fit ladder), never by compressing, stretching, or slanting letterforms. A role chosen for a narrow or tilted form is given room for it.

## Quality checks

**Gate checks for this step (fails if any is false):**
- Color, type, and mark each show their comparison (apparent option named, options, decisive criterion). Palette and type were each compared against the Environment Profile's surroundings and light, and the comparison is recorded. The value key supports every quality requirement, and color intensity follows rank.
- Type has a stated voice drawn from this subject. Roles in one group differ on at least one named typographic attribute other than scale (classification, weight, width, posture, case, spacing, color), and same-role items share one style. Font names appear only for a stated brand requirement. Each text role has a decided width class, posture, baseline, spacing, and alignment.
